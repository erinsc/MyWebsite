Nickel

I'm sure you're aware of the process of Fission, where a very heavy atom like Uranium-235 splits into smaller elements, in this case Krypton-92 and Barium-141, and with those releases 3 extra neutrons and a looot of energy. Put uranium into a power-plant, now you can power a million homes with a rock the size of a fist for a year. Put the rock inside a bomb, and you can vaporize a city. You're also likely heard of Fusion, which is the reverse. inside the sun, atoms of Hydrogen to make Helium, also making energy.

So, since both splitting big, and merging small atoms makes energy, surely somewhere in the middle there's a most stable element. and that's Iron! In particular Iron-56 is the "mid point", where its not possible to make any more energy using it. This is why iron is so common inside planets; as stars use up their Hydrogen, they use Helium. when that runs out, they keep fusing heavier and heavier elements until they get to Iron. anything heavier than Iron gets fissed back down, so the star just slowly makes more and more until it cant keep itself stable and explodes, leaving it behind.

BUT! there's one catch: Nickel-62. Though its bigger, its nucleus is bound ever so slightly tighter, meaning it takes even more energy to make, the most of any Element or Isotope. Its not as common as iron, the atom has to be lucky to get to those 62, but once it gets there it stays. Its believed that the cores of all 4 inner planets are made of almost pure Iron, with a few percents of Nickel.

the 2019 SI unit revision

Before the 2019 SI revision, the celsius scale was defined as being the kelvin scale, + exactly 273.15, and 1 Kelvin was defined as a measure of internal heat, equal to 1/273.16 (yes .16, not .15) of the triple point of "water".

after the revision, its defined with a giant equation involving a ton of seemingly random numbers, and a bunch of universal constants, namely h, the Planck constant, k, the Boltzmann constant, and Δv_Cs, the measured hyperfine structure transition frequency of a Caesium-133 atom.

all of these are "universal" constants. not universal as in useable anywhere, but literally pertaining to the universe itself. the planck constant is from quantum physics, botzmanns from statistics etc.

all these numbers are extremely specific and weird looking, but thats because they were selected precisely so that the values from the new definition were as close to the ones before the revision. can you imagine the chaos if the value of Kelvin suddenly changed lol. there were some differences however. the most significant one was the Ampere, whose value increased by about a millionth. not enough to care for most of us, but precise measurement devices had to adjust

Boiling point of water

The Celcius temperature scale was first defined in 1742 albeit backwards from what we know it now. Water was said to boil at 0˚C and freeze at 100˚C. As simple and clear this is, at the time a lot of physical things weren't yet understood or considered. the temperatures are affected by pressure, contaminants, and even the precise ratio of isotopes. At the extreme end, water made from only deuterium atoms can have a 10% higher density than normal water!

Nowadays we want exact precision. As a part of this, a new temperature scale was created, Kelvin, which is absolute, and as of the 2019 SI revision no longer based on physical measurements. Even though they tried to keep it in-line with the original values, they did change slightly. The VSMOW "Standard Water", has no impurities, and a very precise ratio of different isotopes, for example 1 atom of deuterium per 6420 hydrogen atoms. At one standard atmosphere of pressure, this water boils at 99.98˚C, not 100! It still freezes at 0.00˚C tho. Theres some other (very) slight differences, like its density being 0.9998 grams per milliliter at 0˚C, and only 0.9618 grams at 95˚C.

Types of Taste

For a long time people thought there were 4 basic types of taste: Sweet, Sour, Salty and Bitter. Ofcourse there were more flavours, but people thought only these 4 had taste buds on the tongue. However in 1908 a 5th taste bud type was identified, one that responded to amino-acids i.e. the protein-ness of a food. It was discovered at a Japanese university by a Japanese scientist, who was trying to isolate the specific taste of seaweed, so many people use the Japanese term 'umami' to refer to the taste, but you can also use the english translation 'savory', which basically just means 'tasty'. (unsurprisingly, ancient Greeks didn't know that meat tastes good because it's made of proteins lol).

In recent years, more types of taste buds have been identified, and there could be more. For example fatty acid molecules for an oily taste, tannins for a weird "dry" taste such as in tea or red wine, and calcium for "chalkiness". And of course there are tastes that don't have a taste receptor but have a distinct taste, such as capsaicin causing spiciness.

Bitterness

The human tongue is far more reactive to bitter things than to any other taste. It makes sense, since bitter things are often poisons or harmful chemicals, and you don't wanna eat those, but its still wild to see the comparison to others:

  • Saltiness: So far we only know of one receptor that triggers saltiness, which looks for Na+ ions
  • Sweetness: two genes encode for receptors that bind to small sacharides like glucose. They can also be triggered by some other molecules that we can make artificial sweeteners out of
  • Savoriness: two genes encode receptors to detect amino-acids, such as Glutamate. these all have a very regular structure, with a carboxyl group on the end and an amine on the side. they two fit together like legos and let amino-acids make long squiggly chains - proteins!
  • Sourness: Pretty much only looks for H+ ions. Quite literally the more something is acidic, the more sour it is (there are suggestions of more receptors, looking for other ions)
  • Bitterness: encoded by at least 43 genes, each of which encodes dozens of receptors, which can detect thousands of different compounds. the BitterDB website currently has 2250 different bitter molecules catalogued, and there are likely many many more

Superfluids

Helium is the least reactive element on the periodic table. it doesn't want to do anything, at all, ever, except make a pair with another helium buddy. Even condensing He₂ to a liquid requires cooling it to 4 Kelvin, about -269°C. When things get this cold, quantum physics starts being annoying, and atoms are less like points and more like bumps, or waves. instead of moving around their neighbors, they can move through them, which basically means theres no friction.

No friction means no viscosity. no viscosity means that the fluid doesn't care to hold together at all, and wants to spread out as much as possible, which includes climbing walls against gravity. If you had a bowl of liquid helium, it would climb the walls up and pour down on the floor until the beaker was empty, and the entire room - every single nook and cranny in it - was covered by a 1 atom thick layer of helium.

True Flatness

How do you make something flat? like super precise flat? cant use a sander since you could accidentally make it bulge in or out by a few millimeters and not even notice. You dont need lasers, or any kind of magnetic computer doohickeys, just 3 flat rocks using the "Whitworth 3 place method".

  1. rub rocks A and B together until they move perfectly smooth. now A will bulge out by the same amount as B bulges in.
  2. rub A and C together until they move smooth. they'll also complement like before, but now C will also match (almost) exactly B.
  3. rub B and C together until smooth. because they matched, their bulging (almost) perfectly cancel itself other out, making them both flat.
  4. repeat steps 1-3 over and over until you get bored. even in just 2 repeats you can get rocks that are precise down to micrometers

History of Pi

Its Pi day today! If for some reason you write months before days, today is 3.14. which are the first 3 digits of the famous number Pi (if written in base ten at least).

The classic definition of pi is a circle's circumference divided by its diameter. So if you have a circle 5 meters wide, you'll need 5 * Pi meters of rope to circle it exactly. The number is "transcendental", which means there's no way to write it down as a fraction, or even using an algebraic formula. That doesn't stop us from calculating it however, it just takes a lot more effort.

One of the first methods was imagining a circle, and two squares. one square just inside the circle, one just outside, then calculating the lengths of the squares and averaging. then replacing the squares with pentagons, then hexagons, heptagons etc. the more sides you add the closer you get to the circle. The record for number of digits was 38 digits, using a shape that had 10^40 sides, and likely took many years of calculating.

Then came Newton. In the 1660s, when the plague was raging in the streets, he was stuck home, bored, during lockdown. during this time, he and a fellow mathematician invented Calculus, and found a way to write down Pi without using any circles at all, instead using an infinite sum of smaller and smaller values. A few decades later, Machin found a better approximation and calculated 100 digits. The closest approximation without using a calculators was done by William Shanks, which got to 527 digits!

Nowadays we of course use computers to calculate for us. As of November 2025, we've calculated pi to 314 trillion digits. But why? There isnt really any point to knowing more than 10 or so digits. If you calculate the volume of the entire universe with 40 digits of Pi, you'll be off of the actual value by less than a single atom. So all this is just a bit of fun :>

Lagrange points

Usually we calculate the strength of gravity with Newton's equation, which returns a vector - a direction that an object will move if it starts at that point. However a mathematician named Lagrange created his own equations, which give the result as a 2D gradient. its (a lot!) harder to calculate, but it allowed him to notice something very interesting when calculating the gravity of earth and the sun:

Theres 5 points, Lagrange points, in different parts of the system whose gravity is 0. In other words, the gravity of the earth and sun perfectly balance each other, and whatever you put there really just stays there.

L1 is the most obvious, its between the earth and the sun. their gravity cancel each other out. L3, L4, and L5 are pretty simple too, theyre on earths orbit and rotate at the same speed as the earth does L2 is the most interesting: Its directly behind earth. in total darkness from the sun. Its further from the sun, so usually the orbital speed would be slower, but on this point, the gravity of the earth and sun add up, and make the orbital speed of objects here just fast enough to not lag behind.

P=NP

I'm sure if you're interested in computers you've seen this equation at some point before, but what does it mean and why do people care so much?

This isn't actually an equation but a comparison of "Computation classes". P is a class of all problems that "can be solved by a Turing Machine in Polynomial time". In simpler terms, we care about how long it takes a computer to solve a problem, depending on its size. If the maximum number of steps is writable as a polynomial equation, its in P.

NP is a similar class, it contains problems that are verifiable in Polynomial time. If we're given a problem and a possible solution for it, we can check if the solution is correct quickly.

Clearly if we can solve a problem in P, we can also verify it in P. we simply solve the problem again, and check if the solutions match. in other words P =< NP. However, its not known if the opposite is true, if any problem that can be verified in P can also be solved in P, if P = NP. We can only hope that it isn't. Why? Think about how passwords work. Ideally it should be hard for a computer to guess your password, but very easy to check if the password you gave it is correct. if P = NP then the whole concept of passwords stops working :>. That doesn't mean that it would be possible to crack passwords, but it wouldn't be good.

Floppy disks

Even though floppy disks have not been relevant for most people for well over two decades, and no new ones have been made since 2011, they are still being sold and bought in large amounts.

There are still a lot of businesses which can't or don't want to upgrade from them yet and use them for daily work. For example: airplane navigation computers, MRIs, army installations, automated factories.

In 2022, the largest floppy disk seller online sold about 1000 disks every day, and even though they often get "new" disks from businesses getting rid of their stock, theyre expecting to run out completely in a few years, finally ending the day of the floppy

Venus colonisation

When talking about colonising other planets, most people will likely not think of Venus as a viable planet to live on. Its an extremely hot, dense planet, with acid rain pouring daily, and days 100x longer than earth. In some ways however, its an ideal location!

Turns out, if you look slightly above the surface, around 50km up, you'll find that the atmosphere is a comfortable 1hPa, around 25°C, and since its above the first layer of clouds theres no rain, and theres still enough atmosphere to protect you from space radiation. The winds can also carry you around the planet giving you much shorter days, around 4x as on earth. The atmosphere also has CO2 in it, which you can use to grow plants to get oxygen and food!

Its not perfect of course, you'd need to find a way to keep a base afloat for years on end. you cant bring in resources from the ground, so you have to get them from space, and while its not raining acid, the acid is still in there so you have to wear a suit, albeit not a pressurised one.

Avatar (the blue one)

James Cameron wanted his avatar movie to be extremely realistic and accurate, and he spend a lot of time and money to make sure. Case in point: the spaceship ISV Venture Star. The spaceship shows up on screen for only a few minutes at the beginning of the first movie. but its design is actually very well thought out and completely realistic and functional! There are only two main issues preventing us from building it

  1. it uses anti-matter for fuel. we can make anti-matter already but only in small amounts.
  2. it uses room-temperature superconductors to store that anti-matter.

As far as we know this material doesn't exist yet, but if it did it would lead an incredible tech revolution in basically every industry ever. from levitating rails to efficient computers, to spaceships. You probably don't remember, but this is actually what the main motivation of humans is in the movie! Pandora's flying mountains can fly because they're made of room-temperature superconductors.

Pigeons

Its not their usual stereotype, but pigeons are one of the smartest birds out there. They have incredible ability to recognise faces, both human and pigeon, as well as patterns, to the point that researchers have been able to train them to recognise cancer cells better than AI models.

The density of neurons in their brain is some of the highest in nature, apparently over 3 times denser than in a human brain!

Fun fact #8 - RNA Life

The vast majority of functional stuff inside your cells is made of proteins, tiny factories that process stuff into other stuff, and make basically everything you're made of. But there are a few exceptions, including one giant one: The proteins themselves

Instead, proteins are made by ribosomes, which is primarily composed of RNA. The ribosome reads a chain of messenger RNA, and uses it as instructions to combine little pieces of transfer RNA that carry amino acids, and knits them together into proteins. In short, RNA reads RNA to combine RNA to make proteins.

Many researchers today take this as evidence that the first life on the planet was just simple RNA molecules, floating freely through a chemical soup. Over time these evolved and protected themselves by creating cells to hide in, like tiny armored tanks. While this isn't yet proven, all components of RNA have been recently found in space on asteroids!

Negative Colour

In 1931 researchers conducted a "colour matching experiment", where people had to mix 3 beams of light - red, green and blue - so that it matched a different "target" light. The goal was to find out how strongly different frequencies of light affect the light-cones inside our eyes. But they noticed something weird:

For green-ish and cyan-ish colours, the participants couldn't ever match the colour. To fix this, the researchers allowed people to add red light to the target, which basically means adding negative red light to the colour mix. This fixed the experiment.

This doesn't mean that there's actual negative red, just that you can't mix certain cyan colours with just blue and green. Your computer screen can't make cyan and green as saturated as red or blue.

Definition of a desert
